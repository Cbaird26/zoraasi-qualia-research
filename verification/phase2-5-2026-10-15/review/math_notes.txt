A. Static-prior arbitration: plausible mechanism, incomplete causal diagnosis
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
The formula describing the old blend is correct: p=w f+(1-w)m. But the cited
0.185283 versus 0.172124 results came from the corrected benchmark, whose m was
estimated from independent training trials, not fixed exactly at 7/9. The earlier
uploaded engine benchmark did use oracle initialization m=7/9. Both used a
static Regime-A marginal; they must not be conflated numerically.

For an outcome Y~Bernoulli(q), conditional on information available before Y:

  E[(p-Y)^2] = q(1-q)+(p-q)^2.

Let alpha=1-w. Relative to the unblended forecast f, the exact Brier-risk change is

  Delta = 2 alpha(f-q)(m-f) + alpha^2(m-f)^2.

If f=q exactly, Delta=alpha^2(m-q)^2, not simply (m-q)^2. For an imperfect f,
the cross term can have either sign, so blending may improve or worsen loss.
With estimated forecasts, both bias and variance matter. Removing the old
arbitration also changes errors and subsequent updates, so the aggregate
ablation difference is not a direct measurement of this single bias term.

There is no theorem that shrinkage "must" target 0.5 or an online marginal.
Counterexample in old Regime B: q=f=0.82 after a zero, w=0.5.

  no blending:                  Brier .147600000000; BCE .471393486810
  blend with old m=7/9:          Brier .148045679012; BCE .472818984365
  blend with neutral m=0.5:      Brier .173200000000; BCE .534908403096

Here neutral shrinkage is worse than the stale marginal, and both worsen a
correct conditional estimate. Neutral shrinkage can be less harmful for q=.18,
but that does not guarantee a net benefit across states, environments or
estimation errors. A fast online marginal still ignores the previous-state
conditional probability. Removing or changing arbitration is a testable design
choice, not a mathematically guaranteed improvement.

The new engine removes the blend entirely. Its online marginal serves the
valence calculation only. It is updated with Y_t BEFORE its log loss on Y_t is
computed, while the engine probability was formed before Y_t. This makes the
baseline comparison optimistic for the marginal: if m'=(1-eta)m+eta Y_t,
(m'-Y_t)^2=(1-eta)^2(m-Y_t)^2, and its log loss also decreases for eta>0.
Thus the valence advantage is not a fair comparison of two pre-outcome forecasts.
This does not leak Y_t into the engine's scored p_t; it biases a controller
quantity used for later adaptation/consolidation.

B. Synchronous donors: a confounded control, not proven timing leakage
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Donor and recipient share the latent regime schedule, so their numerical states
may contain relevant common regime information. This weakens their use as an
independence null. It does not imply their random prediction errors, arousal
levels or continuity changes spike at exactly the same steps, or even spike at
every switch. Errors depend on realized outcomes, previous states, parameter
estimates, and prior controller history. A smoothed arousal measure can lag.

There is a more direct implementation error in the proposed explanation: the
old Phase 2.4 engine does not read stored state.continuity anywhere in prediction
or update. It recomputes new_continuity from the target's fast/slow parameters
and current error before retrieval/consolidation. Transferring the donor's old
continuity therefore cannot directly trigger the old recipient's retrieval.
Changing only stored continuity between 0 and 1 yielded identical predictions
and identical updated states in the executed 256-path check.

In the old corrected benchmark, donor scalar vectors were copied BEFORE any
current outcome was exposed. They contain donor observations only through
t-1. The recipient's current target Y_t never leaked into its scored p_t. The
issue was shared regime information and mixed scalar pathways; calling it
"synchronous schedule leakage" without qualification overstates the evidence.
Confidence can affect old p_t immediately; arousal/valence can affect later
updates. The prior continuity scalar has no such effect.

The new Phase 2.5 control changes the chronology and intervention:

  1. Donor scalar trace entry t is computed using donor outcome at t.
  2. Recipient p_t is computed before the donor override and before updating.
  3. Donor scalar entry t controls recipient updating and memory decisions.
  4. Those changes can affect recipient p_(t+1), not its scored p_t.

Actual-code perturbation checks confirm this: replacing low versus high donor
arousal leaves current p_t=.92 unchanged, but changes next p_(t+1) from .9216 to
.9472 in a simple repeated-one example. Changing donor outcome t produced two
recipient paths [.92, .928137043690] and [.92, .924069387838].

Precomputing a donor trace is not by itself future-data leakage; entries used
here are prefix-computable. This chronology is causal if donor outcomes at t
are available before recipient prediction t+1. However, it is not the same
intervention as the previous pre-outcome state replacement. New code uses donor
continuity directly for gating, and stores endogenous scalars afterward rather
than making the donor values its persistent scalar state.

A fixed offset is a phase-mismatch control, not independent stochastic timing.
The supplied Phase 2.5 code does not implement random dwell times. Its listed
desynchronized schedule has a final 23-step B block immediately after a 45-step
B block, creating one 68-step B dwell. It is not a strictly periodic offset
through the end. Neither this control nor the previous synchronized comparison
isolates the causal contribution of each scalar without component ablations.

C. Initialization: qualitative explanation broadly right; table mostly wrong
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Use known p01=a=.28, p11=b=.92, lambda=.64, pi=7/9, H=29 and q0=P(X0=1).
These expectations exclude finite-training estimation error. "Asymptotic" must
distinguish the parameter-known/training-limit reference at fixed H from the
pointwise time limit t->infinity.

Define
  mu_t = pi+(q0-pi)lambda^t,
  r_j(t) = pi+(j-pi)lambda^t,
  B(q,p)=q(1-p)^2+(1-q)p^2,
  C(q,p)=-q log(p)-(1-q)log(1-p).

For either loss L=B or C:
  reset(t)=(1-q0)L(r_0(t),a)+q0 L(r_1(t),b),
  deranged(t)=(1-mu_(t-1))L(mu_t,a)+mu_(t-1)L(mu_t,b).

The JSON also includes stateful/memoryless exact references, all time steps,

The quoted single-state time-limit Brier values are correctly rounded:
  conditioning on initial 1: B(pi,.92)=.193066666667;

Corresponding BCE values are .626125394577 and 1.063085318293. The reset time
limit weights them by the ORIGINAL q0, whereas the deranged donor marginal
converges to pi. Thus pointwise reset limits are:

  q0        reset Brier(t->infinity)   reset BCE(t->infinity)
  1         .193066666667             .626125394577
  7/9       .243634567901             .723227599847
  .5        .306844444444             .844605356435
  0         .420622222222            1.063085318293

For EVERY q0, pointwise deranged Brier tends to .243634567901 and BCE to
.723227599847, not .2504. The 29-step averages are distinct from these limits.
As the averaging horizon itself tends to infinity, the same limiting averages
hold. For the pointwise limits, reset=deranged precisely at q0=pi.

Finite-H crossover: replace "substantially below pi" with an explicit boundary.
For H=29, define Delta(q0)=mean reset loss - mean deranged loss. Numerically:

  Delta_Brier(q0)=.047845995701062 q0^2
                  -.236593289097748 q0 + .141510888802393;
  Delta_BCE(q0)=.126597837368120 q0^2
                -.476691174279626 q0 + .258290994601492.

Each has one root in [0,1]:
  Brier: q0*=.696113688294747;
  BCE:   q0*=.656197028823349.

Reset is worse below the respective boundary and better above it; equality
holds at the boundary. Between .656197 and .696114, the two metrics give
opposite rank orders. The numerical polynomial representation was checked
against the direct risk formulas at additional initial probabilities, and
the signs were checked on each side of each root.
